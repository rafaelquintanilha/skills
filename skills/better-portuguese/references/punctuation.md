# Punctuation

This is the canonical reference for the punctuation prescriptions used by
Better Portuguese. Agents use it when drafting and reviewing sentence-level
prose; contributors use the same reference when changing the editorial
standard or its evals.

The cited prescriptions determine grammatical admissibility. Editorial review
may identify overuse or an artificial AI construction, but it must not
contradict the grammar. First determine what the punctuation mark does in the
sentence; then decide whether the construction should be rewritten for
editorial reasons.

## Bibliographic reference

ALMEIDA, Napoleão Mendes de. *Gramática metódica da língua portuguesa*. Rio de
Janeiro: Ed. CDB, 2024. 800 p. ISBN 978-65-5481-061-6.

The supplied catalog record does not identify an ordinal edition number.
References therefore identify the 2024 CDB publication, the numbered paragraph,
and the printed page. The consulted material is Chapter 65, "Pontuação",
pp. 614-627, §§947-975.

The source scans are research evidence and are not part of this repository.
The descriptions below paraphrase the prescriptions in English; Portuguese
technical labels are retained where useful.

Short examples are transcribed from the source so that each prescription can
be understood in context. They document the rule; their subject matter,
vocabulary, and cadence are not automatically recommendations for Better
Portuguese.

## Punctuation as a system

### Definition and classification

Source: §§947-948, p. 614.

- Punctuation divides parts of discourse that do not have an intimate
  syntactic connection and makes the relationships among those parts clearer.
- The book classifies the marks as *objetivas* (comma, semicolon, colon, and
  period), *subjetivas* (question mark, exclamation mark, ellipsis, and
  parentheses), and *distintivas* (quotation marks, dash, paragraph mark,
  braces, brackets, and asterisk).

## Comma

### Governing principles

Source: §949, p. 615.

- A comma indicates a pause, but a pause in speech does not by itself authorize
  a comma.
- Do not separate a subject from its verb, a verb from its complement, or other
  terms joined by an intimate syntactic relationship merely because speech
  permits a pause.
- Where there is no pause, there is no comma.
- When a pair of commas performs the work of parentheses around an inserted
  expression, both commas must be present or both must be absent.

Source example:

> `Pedro, de acordo com as ordens recebidas, partiu.`

The two commas delimit the inserted expression. Omitting only one would break
the relation between the subject `Pedro` and the verb `partiu`.

### Coordinated elements and clauses

Source: §950, pp. 616-617.

- Use commas among words, sentence members, or clauses with the same syntactic
  function, including multiple subjects, objects, and asyndetic independent
  clauses.
- An additive or alternative conjunction may replace the final comma in a
  coordinated series.
- A comma may accompany repeated `e` or `ou` when the repetition deliberately
  emphasizes each coordinated element.
- When `ou` states equivalence between two terms, either omit punctuation or
  delimit the equivalent expression with a comma before `ou` and another after
  it.
- A comma before `e` is justified when `e` opens an inserted expression,
  separates material governed by different verbs, or connects independent
  clauses whose structures require the separation. It must not be decided by a
  blanket prohibition against a comma before `e`.
- Do not place a comma before `etc.` merely because it closes an enumeration.
- In addresses and similar constructions, punctuation before a number follows
  the number's syntactic relationship to the noun it specifies.

Source examples:

> `A riqueza, a saúde, o prazer são coisas transitórias.`
>
> `Ou ele vá, ou pare, ou retroceda.`
>
> `Disse ele muitas coisas, e mais coisas teria dito não fosse a carência de
> tempo.`
>
> `Caixa postal 44.` / `Rua 23, casa 4.`

The third example permits a comma before `e` because the coordinated material
belongs to different verbs. The address pair contrasts a number that directly
modifies `caixa postal` with a number specifying an omitted `casa`.

### Restrictive adjective clauses

Source: §951, pp. 617-618.

- A comma may mark the end of a very long restrictive adjective subordinate
  clause.
- For a short adjective clause, the distinction between explanatory and
  restrictive function determines whether commas are used.

Source example:

> `As famílias que se estabeleceram naquelas encostas meridionais das longas
> serranias chamadas pelos antigos Montes Marianos, conservaram por mais tempo
> os hábitos...`

The comma closes the unusually long restrictive clause. The book does not
present this as permission to surround every restrictive clause with commas.

### Ambiguity and displaced complements

Source: §952, p. 618.

- Use a comma when necessary to prevent ambiguity caused by inversion or a
  forcefully displaced complement.

Source example:

> `Aqui se levanta ao céu, da gente, o pensamento.`

The commas make `da gente` modify `o pensamento`, rather than leaving its
attachment ambiguous after the inversion.

### Dates

Source: §953, p. 618.

- Separate the place name from the date that follows it.

Source example:

> `Itajaí, 8 de janeiro de 1911.`

### Zeugma

Source: §954, p. 618.

- Use a comma to mark an omitted verb recoverable from the parallel
  construction.

Source example:

> `Os valorosos levam as feridas, os venturosos, os prêmios.`

The second comma occupies the position of the omitted `levam`.

### Parallel proverbial expressions

Source: §955, p. 618.

- Use a comma to separate parallel members of a proverbial expression.

Source example:

> `Cada terra com seu uso, cada roca com seu fuso.`

### Postpositive conjunctions

Source: §956, pp. 618-619.

- Delimit postpositive conjunctions such as `porém`, `contudo`, `pois`, and
  `todavia` when they function as inserted elements.
- These conjunctions can occur without commas when their syntactic role does
  not require parenthetical separation.

Source examples:

> `Naquele dia, porém, as lanças...`
>
> `Desempenham todavia funções...`

The contrast shows why the word itself does not mechanically determine the
punctuation.

### Emphatic adverbs and adverbial expressions

Source: §957, p. 619.

- Commas may delimit conjunctions, adverbs, and adverbial expressions when the
  writer intends them to carry marked emphasis.
- Do not systematically put an initial conjunction or every adverbial
  expression between commas. The commas are justified by the inserted,
  emphatic function, not by membership in a word list.

Source examples:

> `Alguém, talvez, queira.`
>
> `Acho isso impossível, pois, estive ontem com ele.`

The book gives the second sentence as an error: the comma after `pois` has no
syntactic or emphatic function there.

### Initial affirmation, negation, and connective adverbs

Source: §§958-959, p. 619.

- Use a comma after sentence-initial `sim` or `não`.
- Use a comma after initial expressions such as `assim`, `então`, and `demais`
  when they function as conjunctions.

Source examples:

> `Sim, quero.` / `Não, porque já foi.`
>
> `Então, iremos hoje?` / `Assim, espero por você.`

### Explanatory expressions

Source: §960, p. 619.

- Delimit explanatory expressions such as `isto é`, `por exemplo`, `por assim
  dizer`, `a meu ver`, `além disso`, `a saber`, and `ou seja`.

Source example:

> `Porei todavia aqui mais um exemplo, isto é, acrescentarei mais uma prova.`

### Vocative

Source: §961, pp. 619-620.

- A vocative is always separated by commas: after it at the beginning, around
  it in the middle, and before it at the end of a clause.

Source examples:

> `Alunos, recordem as correções.`
>
> `Recordem, alunos, as correções.`
>
> `Recordem as correções, alunos.`

### Emphatic omission of `ser`

Source: §962, p. 620.

- A comma may emphatically occupy the place of an omitted form of `ser` when
  the sentence remains readily understood.

Source example:

> `Estes, os maiores perigos.`

### Parentheses at a comma boundary

Source: §963, p. 620.

- When a parenthetical insertion occupies a position where the surrounding
  sentence requires a comma, put the comma after the closing parenthesis if it
  belongs to the material that precedes the insertion.

Source example:

> `Estava em sua casa (nenhum prazer sentia fora dela), quando ouviu baterem.`

The comma remains necessary after the parenthesis because it belongs to the
surrounding period.

## Semicolon

Source: §965, pp. 620-621.

- The semicolon has greater separating force than a comma and less than a
  period. The book describes the comma as separating concepts, ideas, and
  phrases; the semicolon as separating judgments or clauses; and the period as
  ending the reasoning or grammatical period.
- Use a semicolon between independent clauses of some length, especially when
  those clauses already contain commas.
- Use it between the principal divisions of a sentence when their subordinate
  divisions are already separated by commas.
- In the preamble of certain legal or administrative documents, use it between
  the `considerandos`, except after the last one.

Source examples:

> `Das graças que há no mundo, as mais sedutoras são as da beleza; as mais
> picantes, as do espírito; as mais comoventes, as do coração.`
>
> `Santos, Campinas, Recife são cidades do Brasil; Madri, Sevilha, Barcelona,
> da Espanha; Lisboa, Porto, Coimbra, de Portugal.`

The first separates independent clauses that already contain commas. The
second separates the principal divisions of an enumeration whose subordinate
items also require commas.

The supplied sequence moves from §963 to §965; no §964 appears in the scanned
material.

## Colon

Source: §966, pp. 621-622.

- Use a colon to introduce a quotation.
- Use it to announce or present an enunciation or enumeration, whether the
  presented material follows or precedes the verb or presenting expression.
  The construction may also be verbless.
- Use it before a reflection or explanation.
- In the specified legal construction, use it between the preamble and the
  final item in a series of `considerandos`.
- In the enunciation/enumeration and reflection/explanation uses, begin the
  following material with a lowercase letter.

Source examples:

> Citation: `Aristóteles dizia a seus discípulos: Meus amigos, não há amigos.`
>
> Enumeration after its presentation: `Os meios legítimos de adquirir fortuna
> são três: trabalho, ordem e economia.`
>
> Enumeration before its presentation: `Trabalho, ordem e economia: eis os
> meios legítimos de adquirir fortuna.`
>
> Explanation: `Não o moleste: é um pobre coitado.`
>
> Legal preamble: `Tomando em consideração o relatório do Ministro e Secretário
> dos Negócios da Fazenda: Hei por bem decretar...`

The lowercase note applies to the enumeration and explanation examples. It
does not create a general rule that every passage following a colon begins
with lowercase.

These grammatical permissions do not by themselves endorse using a colon as a
manufactured reveal or punch line. That is a separate editorial judgment.

## Period and paragraph flow

### Period

Source: §967, pp. 622-623.

- A period concludes a grammatical period.
- No fixed grammatical rule determines the ideal division or length of
  periods; the decision belongs substantially to the writer's style and
  judgment.
- Avoid both exhausting classical periods and a monotonous multiplication of
  excessively short periods. Good prose coordinates and subordinates clauses
  into a harmonious, fluent whole.

The section deliberately gives no numerical target or model sentence. Its
example is comparative: very long classical periods can tire the reader, but a
succession of excessively short periods also becomes tedious. The criterion is
whether subordination and coordination produce a fluent, natural whole.

### Continue the line or begin a new one

Source: §968, p. 623.

- Let the sequence of thought determine whether a new grammatical period stays
  on the same line or begins a new one.
- Begin on a new line when there is a cut in thought. Remain on the same line
  when the new period continues or follows from the previous one.

The use case is semantic rather than typographic: a new sentence does not by
itself require a new paragraph. The following period remains in the same
paragraph when it continues the same movement of thought.

## Question mark

Source: §969, p. 623.

- Put a question mark at the end of a direct question.
- A question mark does not always end the grammatical period. When a reporting
  clause continues the same period after the question, continue with lowercase.

Source examples:

> `Quem quer ir?`
>
> `Quem quer ir? perguntou o chefe.`

The second example shows that the question mark can perform its interrogative
function without ending the grammatical period.

## Exclamation mark

Source: §970, pp. 623-624.

- Use an exclamation mark after an interjection or a clause expressing such
  states as astonishment, admiration, or surprise.
- Question and exclamation marks may be combined when the expression carries
  both question and admiration.
- Apply the same continuation principle described for the question mark when a
  reporting clause follows within the same grammatical period.
- Distinguish vocative `ó`, which does not itself require an exclamation mark,
  from interjective `oh!`.
- Portuguese does not adopt the inverted opening question or exclamation marks
  used in Spanish.

Source examples:

> `Quanto peixe!`
>
> `Meu filho! exclama a pobre mulher.`
>
> `Que?!`

The second example follows the same continuation rule as a question followed
by a reporting clause. The third combines question and exclamation.

## Ellipsis

Source: §971, p. 624.

- Ellipsis marks interruption or suspension of thought, hesitation, or the
  decision that completing the thought is unnecessary.
- A complete line of points may indicate words or lines omitted from a copied
  or translated work.

Source examples:

> `Se tu soubesses... oh! nada...`
>
> `Se ele é bom, ela...`

The first marks hesitation and interruption; the second leaves a parallel
thought unexpressed because the reader can recover it.

## Parentheses

Source: §972, pp. 624-625.

- Parentheses separate explanatory words or phrases inserted into a grammatical
  period.
- Read the inserted material in a lower tone.
- Begin parenthetical material with a capital only when it forms a complete,
  independent sentence or thought; in that case, the parenthesis is ordinarily
  preceded by a period.
- Short inserted phrases are generally better delimited by commas. Excessively
  long parentheses impair the clarity of the period.
- Parentheses can enclose numbers, dates, letters, and asterisks. A closing
  parenthesis alone can mark items in a sequence such as `a)`, `b)`, and `c)`.

Source example:

> `Estava Mário em sua casa (nenhum prazer sentia fora dela) quando ouviu
> baterem...`

The inserted clause is explanatory and can be removed without breaking the
surrounding sentence.

## Quotation marks

### Principal uses

Source: §973, p. 625.

- Put quotation marks at the beginning and end of quoted material.
- Use them to distinguish words and expressions foreign to the surrounding
  vocabulary.
- Use them to call attention to a word whose form or meaning is itself under
  discussion.
- Typographic emphasis such as italics or another distinct typeface can replace
  quotation marks when it already performs that function.

Source examples:

> Quotation: `Um sábio disse: "Agir na paixão é embarcar durante a
> tempestade".`
>
> Foreign expression: `Pedro vive num verdadeiro "dolce far niente".`
>
> Word under discussion: `A palavra "mandar" nem sempre significa o mesmo que
> "enviar".`

### Nested quotation and punctuation

Source: §973.1, p. 626.

- Use single quotation marks for a quotation nested inside a double-quoted
  passage.
- Quotation marks enclose punctuation that belongs to the quoted passage.
- At the end of a quotation, place punctuation inside the quotation marks when
  it belongs to the quotation and outside when it belongs to the surrounding
  authorial sentence.

Source examples:

> `O mestre avisou: "O aluno que responder 'Não estudei' deverá justificar a
> resposta".`
>
> `Ele perguntou: "Você vai?"`
>
> `Por que você não disse "Eu vou"?`

The question mark belongs to the quoted question in the second example and to
the surrounding authorial question in the third.

## Dash

Source: §974, pp. 626-627.

- The dash is longer than a hyphen and marks a change of interlocutor in
  dialogue.
- It can substitute for parentheses, commas, or colons.
- It can prevent repetition of a term already mentioned, a use common in
  dictionaries.

Source examples:

> Dialogue:
>
> `— Quem é?`
>
> `— Sou eu.`
>
> In place of parentheses: `A la fé — disse Mem Moniz — que a festa de vossos
> anos, senhor Gonçalo Mendes, será...`
>
> In place of commas: `Não fora eu — que há muito a acompanhava — e ela teria
> perecido.`
>
> In place of a colon: `Prestem atenção — Ninguém deve sair.`

The source therefore recognizes legitimate grammatical uses of the dash.
Whether Better Portuguese should avoid particular English-influenced or
overused dash constructions is a separate editorial decision.

## Paragraph

Source: §975, p. 627.

- In dictation, `parágrafo` or `alínea` instructs the writer to begin the next
  period on a new line.
- A paragraph can contain one or more grammatical periods and develops a
  thought or group of thoughts less intimately related to the preceding
  paragraph than the periods within it are related to one another.
- No secure general rule determines paragraph division; outside forms governed
  by their subject or legal structure, it depends on the writer's judgment and
  style.
- The paragraph symbol `§` is mainly associated with subdivisions in legal
  texts and is rare in ordinary prose.

The section's own definition is the operative example: a paragraph may contain
one or more grammatical periods, but those periods form a thought or group of
thoughts more closely related to one another than to the preceding paragraph.
Paragraphing therefore cannot be reduced to a sentence count.
